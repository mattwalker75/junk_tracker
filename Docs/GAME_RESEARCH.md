# Reward the comeback, never punish the log

Dozens of gamified apps reward exercise and healthy habits. **Almost none punish junk food**, and most of the long-lived ones punish nothing at all. Movement games such as Zombies, Run!, Pikmin Bloom, Walkr and Ring Fit Adventure simply pay out nothing on a day with no activity. Habit apps cover the full range: Finch has no penalty, Duolingo resets a streak, Forest and Plant Nanny let a tree or plant die, Habitica takes HP, and Beeminder and StepBet take real money. On the diet side, the pet apps on the market (BitePal, Snapmunch, Capy Diet) reward good meals and leave junk food neutral. A junk-food penalty appears only as a user feature request and in one open-source hobby project. The research agrees with that market. Losing points you were handed at the start of a week beats earning the same points, but harsh opt-in penalties are mostly declined. A broken streak lowers motivation unless it can be repaired. A single "rule break" can set off a rest-of-the-day binge in restrained eaters. Self-criticism tracks disordered eating, and anything that makes logging a Feast costly teaches people to stop logging Feasts. For Junk Tracker, a mild consequence is both possible and fairly new, as long as it meets five conditions: it is small, it happens to something in the game world rather than to the person, it can be repaired by the next good action, it resets every week, and it is never worth avoiding by skipping a log. Every gamified effect fades, typically within 2 to 6 weeks, so the game also needs a planned "second wind" rather than a big launch.

## Most apps pay for effort and simply withhold rewards on a lapse

The apps that keep people for months share one structure: **effort earns a currency, and the currency builds something the player owns and watches grow**. In Zombies, Run! you collect supplies during audio-story runs, then spend them on buildings that raise Abel Township's defence, morale and population. The base does not decay when you stop running ([ZR support](https://support.zombiesrungame.com/hc/en-us/articles/4421007214353-Base-Builder)). Walkr turns steps into energy that speeds up discovering planets for a collection ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id834805518)). Pikmin Bloom keeps players coming back with a steady stream of collectibles: themed Decor Pikmin almost every month through 2025, and storage raised from 3,200 to 4,000 as long-term players filled it ([Pikmin Bloom news](https://pikminbloom.com/news/dec25-storageupgrade/)). Ring Fit Adventure makes exercises the attacks in an RPG. A reviewer who played about five days a week for months said it "did get me motivated to move more" ([iMore](https://www.imore.com/ring-fit-adventure-review)). Fitocracy is the closest precedent to Junk Tracker. It ran entirely on hand-logged workouts scored as XP, with levels and quests, and reached **1 million users by 2013**. It has been offline since 2024, and no reason was ever published ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitocracy)).

Consistency rewards hold up better than performance rewards. Strava created Local Legends because few riders could ever win a speed crown. The title goes to whoever logs the most efforts on a segment over a rolling 90 days, so ordinary athletes can win it ([Cycling Weekly](https://cyclingweekly.com/news/latest-news/strava-unveil-new-segment-feature-that-lets-you-a-top-leaderboards-without-being-fastest-457564)). Pokémon GO's Adventure Sync pays weekly tiers at 5, 25 and 50 km. A missed day only lowers the weekly total ([mein-mmo](https://mein-mmo.de/en/pokemon-go-when-will-the-weekly-reward-arrive-time-content-eggs,412079/)).

Penalties exist in three forms, and each has been softened over time. The first is the **streak break**. Apple Watch users describe pacing their living rooms at night to save a ring streak and feeling "bullied" by it ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/well/2025/01/24/apple-watch-bullied-burn-calories-close-rings-obsession-fitness-trackers-notifications/)). watchOS 11 (2024) added pause days that keep the streak intact ([9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2024/12/31/watchos-11-fixes-easier-apple-watch-health-goals/)). Duolingo now lets learners hold two Streak Freezes at once, and says openly that it designs the streak around fear of loss ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit)). The second is **game-stat loss**. In Habitica, missed Dailies cost HP. At zero HP you lose a level, all your gold and one item. "Rest in the Inn" pauses the damage for illness or travel ([Habitica Wiki: Death](https://habitica.fandom.com/wiki/Death); [Rest in the Inn](https://habitica.fandom.com/wiki/Rest_in_the_Inn)). In party boss quests, every member's misses damage everyone, and the fan wiki itself advises struggling players to stay out of them ([Habitica Wiki: Obstacles](https://habitica.fandom.com/wiki/Obstacles)). The third is **real money**. Beeminder charges you when you fall below your line. It also lets you reply to call a charge "not legit", and it will believe whatever reason you give ([Beeminder help](https://help.beeminder.com/article/16-what-is-a-legit-derailment)). In StepBet you put about $40 into a shared pot for 6 weeks and lose it if you miss your goals ([WayBetter science](https://waybetter.app/science)).

Visible-death designs sit in the middle of that range. Forest's tree dies if you leave the app mid-session, and in Plant Together everyone's tree dies if one person leaves ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/au/app/forest-focus-for-productivity/id866450515)). Plant Nanny's plant wilts if you don't log water. One reviewer kept drinking mainly to keep it alive and admitted "an odd sense of guilt if the plant dies" ([Hilltop Views](https://hilltopviewsonline.com/17554/uncategorized/plant-nanny-app-reminds-users-to-hydrate-regularly-keep-plants-alive)). Finch sits at the opposite end: "There's no punishment for the bird so you can restart the next morning." Reviewers praise how it reduces guilt, but warn that "the reward loop can turn into a chore" over months ([Headway](https://makeheadway.com/blog/finch-app-review/)).

**Food is the gap.** BitePal rewards healthy eating with coins for its raccoon's accessories. Its developer welcomed, but has not shipped, a user's idea that the pet should get "sick" after junk food ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/bitepal-food-tracker-pet/id6479529917)). Snapmunch fills a "Health Potion Meter" with balanced meals and does nothing for bad ones ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/tt/app/snapmunch/id6746213339)). The only real junk-reactive design found is Forki, an open-source iOS project whose pet "grows, thrives, or fades" with your eating habits ([GitHub](https://github.com/Bingia01/Forki)).

The activity gains these apps produce are real but short-lived. Pokémon GO added **about 955 steps a day in its first week, and the gain had disappeared by about week 6** ([Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/57198-pokemon-go-walking-fizzled.html)). A meta-analysis of 16 randomised trials found gamification added about 1,600 steps a day (g = 0.42). About 14 weeks after the programmes ended, the effect had shrunk to g = 0.15 ([JMIR 2022](https://jmir.org/2022/1/e26779)). Fish'n'Steps, an early research prototype that tied a virtual fish's growth and expression to the user's daily steps, saw enthusiasm drop after two weeks. Its authors advised games to "encourage rather than use negative reinforcement" ([UCSB record](https://www.cdgr.ucsb.edu/node/1025); [summary](https://typeset.io/papers/fish-n-steps-encouraging-physical-activity-with-an-2a7enirl7j)).

## The research favours small, pre-given losses and penalties that can be repaired

The strongest case for any "punishment" comes from Penn's loss-framing trials, and it is narrower than it looks. In Patel et al. (2016), overweight adults who were **given $42 a month up front and lost $1.40 for each missed day met their step goal on about 45% of days**. Those who earned $1.40 a day met it on 35% of days, a lottery group on 36% and controls on 30% ([Penn Today](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/2016-03-24/research/prospect-losing-money-pushes-people-achieve-fitness-goals)). The STEP UP trial applied the same idea with points: 70 points at the start of each week, minus 10 for each missed day, plus a ramp-up period and fresh starts ([ACC](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/articles/2019/11/26/08/23/key-takeaways-regarding-gamification-to-increase-physical-activity)). The loss that works is **losing something the game gave you**, not a penalty on top of nothing. Even that effect disappeared when the incentive ended.

Penalties people must opt into mostly go unused. When smokers could choose, about 90% accepted a rewards programme, compared with 13.7% for a refundable-deposit programme. Overall, deposits produced lower abstinence (10.2% vs 15.7%). They worked better only among the minority willing to accept them ([NEJM](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20201218032310/http://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1414293)). An optional "hard mode" in Junk Tracker would likely face the same choice: few would pick it, and those who did would drop out.

The best-performing idea in the evidence is a reward rather than a punishment. In Milkman's megastudy of 61,000 gym members and 54 interventions, the winner paid **a tiny bonus, about 9 cents, for coming back after a missed workout**, framed as "don't miss twice". It raised visits by 27% ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/podcast/knowledge-at-wharton-podcast/how-science-can-help-you-keep-your-fitness-goal/)). Only about 8% of the interventions still had a measurable effect after four weeks ([WEF](https://www.weforum.org/stories/health-and-healthcare-systems/science-fitness-goal-motivation/)). Variable rewards added little. Patel's lottery group did no better than a fixed reward, which argues against building the game around slot-machine surprises.

Streaks work, and they are fragile. Silverman and Barasch ran seven studies. An intact streak in a log raised later engagement and a broken one lowered it, **even when the underlying behaviour was the same**. The drop was larger when people blamed themselves for the break and smaller when the streak could be repaired. The authors advise apps not to dwell on broken streaks ([CU Boulder](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions); [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation)). Fresh-start research explains why Mondays and the first of the month help. Diet searches, gym visits and goal commitments all spike after these landmarks, because they let people file past failures under an earlier period ([Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf)).

The research also points to a specific hazard for a junk-food app. **Restrained eaters who believe they have broken a diet rule go on to eat more**, an effect replicated many times in the lab ([Polivy & Herman 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7096476)), and it usually lasts the rest of the day ([WTOP](https://wtop.com/news/2017/11/avoiding-the-what-the-hell-health-effect)). A "clean streak" that one Snack resets to zero would set this off on purpose. Compassion has the opposite effect. Restrictive eaters given a brief self-compassion message after a diet-breaking doughnut ate less afterwards ([Adams & Leary 2007](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/782823)). Across 135 studies, self-criticism tracks disordered eating ([Paranjothy & Wade 2024](https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-disordered-eating-and-its-association-with-sel/)). Noom's "no forbidden foods" colour system follows the same logic: if no food is forbidden, eating one can't count as breaking a rule ([UMiami Health](https://news.umiamihealth.org/en/is-the-noom-diet-really-a-diet/)).

Honest logging is what the whole design must protect. Self-monitoring predicts weight-loss success, yet energy under-reporting already ranges from 2% to 85% across populations ([Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/361647CA492E2042E8BEF4B936C2D59F/core-reader)). Its strongest predictors are social desirability and dietary restraint ([White Rose/BJN](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/166744/1/BJN%20Revised-%20Identification%20of%20psychological%20corelates%20of%20mis-reporting.pdf)), the same traits a judging app brings out. The risk of eating disorders is real but concentrated in vulnerable users. About 73% of eating-disorder patients who had used MyFitnessPal said it contributed to their disorder ([Levinson et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5700836)). Randomised studies in low-risk users found no causal harm ([Wallace et al. 2025](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/27021/2/wallace-et-al-2025-a-systematic-search-and-review-focusing-on-the-influence-of-health-tracking-technologies-on-eating.pdf)). Design guidance from that literature says to frame exercise around performance and enjoyment rather than calories burned, and to avoid pressing for constant logging ([PMC8485346](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8485346)). Practitioner frameworks agree. Octalysis classes "loss and avoidance" as black-hat motivation, which drives short-term action but breeds resentment ([Yale SOM](https://som.yale.edu/blog/the-motivating-power-of-games)). That is framing, not evidence.

Some questions remain open. No study has tested a penalty on logged junk food. No study has compared a game-world consequence (a sad pet) with a penalty aimed at the person. No formal design guidance from an eating-disorder organisation (NEDA, Beat) was found. The step-goal and gym evidence is solid. Applying it to food is an inference.

## Seven design rules for Junk Tracker's game mode

**Make the consequence temporary and the logging reward permanent.** This rule resolves the tension at the heart of the app. Any cost attached to a logged Feast gives the user some reason not to log it. Simple arithmetic doesn't fix that: with "+1 for logging, −3 for a Feast", skipping the log still comes out ahead. The fix is to keep the two in different currencies. An honest log earns something permanent, such as a stamp, a collectible fragment or a building material. A splurge causes a temporary state in the game world that clears on its own or is cleared by the next good action. Skipping the log avoids a passing state but gives up something lasting, so logging is the better deal. Backdated logs should never be penalised.

**Use a weekly budget that is given in advance and refills on Monday.** This copies the STEP UP structure. The game starts each week full, with something like "calm", "light" or "fuel". Snack, Meal and Feast use up 1, 2 and 3 units. Monday, and the first of each month, refill everything. A running-out state is cosmetic and never destructive: no levels, collections or buildings are ever lost. That avoids Habitica-style death and Plant Nanny-style guilt about a dying creature.

**Make the comeback the biggest reward.** Following the megastudy's "don't miss twice" winner, the first workout or splurge-free day after a Feast, or after a gap in workouts, should earn more than an ordinary day. Frame it as momentum, never as "burning off" the food.

**Count streaks only on positive acts.** Days logged, workouts done and weekly targets met can form streaks. A splurge must never break a streak. Prefer weekly tiers that stack (for example 60/150/300 workout minutes, set from the user's own last four weeks as StepBet does) or a consistency percentage over a single fragile daily chain. Include pause days from day one, as Apple did in 2024, and a "rest in the inn" toggle for illness or travel.

**Pay currency for minutes, not calories, and leave weight out of the score.** Junk Tracker logs calories for activities, but a calorie-based currency turns workouts into payment for food, the compensatory pattern clinicians warn about. Use minutes, perhaps with a variety bonus, and keep weigh-ins as a trend line outside the points economy. The exercise-app notes suggested using weigh-in goals as milestones. The eating-disorder evidence argues against it, and the safer choice is to reward goals about behaviour.

**Write the copy for compassion.** Never use "cheat", "bad", "failed" or "guilty". After a Feast, the message should be something like "Logged — that's the job done. Fresh week Monday." Add a "planned treat" flag that skips the consequence, which mirrors Beeminder's honour-system legit-check. Keep the consequence about the game world, never about the person.

**Keep it optional, adjustable and quiet.** Game mode should be off by default and switched on from the initials menu. It needs a Gentle/Standard intensity choice and a one-tap pause. Send notifications only when nothing has been logged, as the Plant Nanny reviewer wanted, never on a schedule. Plan a second wind around weeks 4 to 6, such as a new area, a new collection or a first "boss" week. A solo app can't match Pikmin Bloom's live content, so it should rely on rotating weekly goals and slowly revealed unlocks.

## Five original game directions built on Junk Tracker's own data

Each direction below uses the app's existing inputs: workout minutes as the currency, Snack/Meal/Feast as a three-step dial for the consequence, and goals as milestones. Each follows the rules above. None copies an existing game's premise. Where one shares a broad mechanic with an existing app, such as building or collecting, the theme, the consequence and the repair work differently.

### The Crumb Goblin: a pest that comedy keeps in check

Instead of a sweet, dependent pet that suffers, which is the guilt engine behind Plant Nanny and the sad step-pets, the creature here *wants* your junk food. A small, cheeky goblin lives in the app's pantry. Each logged splurge gives it a crumb: a Snack makes it smug, a Meal sends it to rummage through cupboards, and a Feast has it throw a little party with streamers. Workouts make it drowsy. Enough minutes in a week and it curls up asleep in a teacup, and that sleeping pose is the weekly "win". Honest logs earn permanent "sightings", a collection of goblin poses and outfits you can only catch by logging, including the party ones. A Feast therefore still adds something to your collection. The consequence is comic mischief, not suffering, so it can't produce Plant Nanny's "odd sense of guilt". The goblin gets a fresh start every Monday. The second wind comes when the goblin's relatives start visiting from week 4.

### Lantern Keeper: workouts light the way, splurges bring fog that lifts

You tend a lighthouse on a stormy coast. Workout minutes become lamp oil, and a brighter beam guides more ships to harbour. Each docked ship leaves a permanent cargo item for your harbour museum. Splurges roll in fog, one band for a Snack, two for a Meal and three for a Feast. Fog dims the beam for a while but sinks nothing and takes nothing back. The next workout or splurge-free day burns the fog off with a bonus "clear night", which is the comeback reward. Every honest log adds a line to the keeper's logbook, which builds into a readable season-long journal. The weekly oil target is set from your own recent minutes, and a stormy-week pause covers illness. Goals unlock new ship types rather than scores.

### The Weather Almanac: your week becomes a page of sky

Each week is a page in an illustrated almanac. Every day's logs paint that day's sky. Workouts add sun, and longer or more varied ones add rarer features such as rainbows, auroras and meteor showers. Splurges add clouds sized by Snack, Meal or Feast. Clouds belong to the weather, not to you: a cloudy Tuesday is just a cloudy Tuesday. On Sunday night the page is bound into the almanac, and on Monday a fresh page opens, which builds the fresh-start effect into the format itself. Honest logging is required for the page to be "complete", which earns a gilt edge. Rewards are rare sky events unlocked by weekly minute tiers that stack. A month's pages form a season with its own cover art. Nothing is scored on weight. This direction suits users who want gentle reflection rather than a creature to manage.

### Clockwork Companion: build a machine, polish off the rust

You assemble a brass automaton one part at a time. Workout minutes become gears and springs, and fitting a full set of parts gives the machine a new ability: it waves, then walks, then plays a tiny instrument. Splurges leave specks of rust on finished parts, from one to three by size. Rust is purely cosmetic and never makes a part fall off. A later workout polishes it away with a satisfying shine, and the first polish after a Feast pays a double gear. Honest logs earn drops of oil, a permanent resource needed for the rarest parts, so a logged Feast still moves the build forward. Ownership and slow construction are the long-term hook, the same reason Zombies, Run!'s base lasts. The parts list can be long enough to give Junk Tracker months of goals without live content.

### The Night Market: run a stall and turn workouts into stock

You run a small lantern-lit stall at a night market. Workout minutes become stock you craft, such as kites, lanterns and spice jars. Customers arrive each evening based on how full your shelves are, and every sale adds to a permanent stall upgrade. A splurge sends a "rain shower" over the market. A Snack is a drizzle and a Feast is a downpour. Fewer customers come that evening, but no stock is lost, and the next good day brings a "clear-sky rush" with extra customers. A "festival night" switch lets the user mark a planned treat, so a birthday dinner becomes a market festival instead of a downpour. Honest logs earn market tokens that unlock new kinds of goods. Weekly targets come from your own baseline, and a new market district opens around week 5 as the second wind.

## Conclusion

The open space for Junk Tracker isn't harsher punishment, which the market tried with money, dying plants and party damage before adding safety valves to all of them. It is a mild consequence for junk food that no one has shipped well. The evidence puts four limits on it. The cost must come out of something the game handed you that week. It must land in the game world rather than on the person. It must be undone by the next good action. And it must never be worth more than the permanent reward for logging honestly. Of the five directions, the Crumb Goblin and the Weather Almanac fit the evidence best, because they remove the dependent-creature guilt that the research and user reports flag. The goblin makes junk food comic rather than shameful, and the almanac turns the fresh start into the structure of the game.

The largest unknown is whether *any* penalty on logged food quietly reduces honest logging over months. No study has tested it, and Junk Tracker can measure it for itself. If splurge logs drop sharply after game mode is switched on while workouts stay steady, the consequence is too strong and the app should soften it automatically. A personal app can afford to tune itself to one user's honesty, which the large engagement-driven apps have never done.
